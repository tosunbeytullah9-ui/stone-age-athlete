The Bajau: Humans Who Evolved Bigger Spleens to Dive
<!-- Pillar P3 · priority A · ~1,450 words · every claim has a line in sources.md -->

Take a deep breath and hold it. Right now. Most of you will want to breathe again in under a minute. Now imagine doing that over and over, all day, while you hunt fish on the sea floor. For the Bajau people, that is not a stunt. It is a working day.

The Bajau are often called the sea nomads of Southeast Asia. They live in the waters around Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines. For generations, many of them lived on houseboats and came to land only to trade. Linguistic studies suggest they have lived this way for more than a thousand years. And when Magellan's expedition crossed these waters in 1521, European sailors wrote about them.

So what does their working day look like? In one field study, Bajau divers spent more than five hours a day underwater. That was about sixty percent of their working time. For comparison, the Japanese Ama divers, famous for their own breath-hold tradition, spent about two hours a day underwater in the same study. The Bajau dive, rise, rest for a few breaths, and dive again. A single dive usually lasts around half a minute. But they repeat it hundreds of times.

They do it with almost no equipment. A spear. A pair of hand-made wooden goggles. No fins, no wetsuit, no air tank. Some reports describe Bajau divers reaching depths of more than seventy metres. Others, looking at ordinary village divers, saw much shallower work, around ten metres. The honest answer is that it varies. But even the shallow version, repeated all day, is something your body is not built for. Or is it?

Here is the question that pulled scientists in. Is this skill, or is it biology? Can anyone who grows up in the water learn to do this? Or has the sea changed the Bajau themselves?

To answer that, you first need to know about a small organ you probably never think about. Your spleen. It sits under your left ribs, about the size of your fist. Most people know it as a filter for the blood. But it also works as a reserve tank. It stores red blood cells, the cells that carry oxygen.

When you hold your breath and put your face in cold water, your body switches into a special mode. Scientists call it the diving response. Your heart slows down. The blood vessels in your arms and legs tighten, so blood stays around your heart and brain. And your spleen squeezes. It pushes its stored red blood cells into the bloodstream. It is like a scuba diver opening a spare oxygen bottle.

You have this response too. Every human does. In trained breath-hold divers, researchers measured the spleen shrinking by about a quarter after a series of breath-holds. That is the spleen emptying its reserve.

Now think about seals. Deep-diving seals, like the Weddell seal, have enormous spleens compared to their body size. A bigger tank means more oxygen on demand. A young geneticist named Melissa Ilardo noticed this and asked a simple question. If seals evolved bigger spleens for diving, could humans do the same?

So she went to Indonesia. She lived in a Bajau village and brought a portable ultrasound machine. She measured the spleens of fifty-nine Bajau people. Then she measured thirty-four people from the Saluan, a neighbouring group that lives on land and does not dive. Same region. Same kind of food. Different life.

The result was striking. The Bajau spleens were about fifty percent larger than the Saluan spleens. By the researchers' estimate, that could mean around ten percent more red blood cells released during a dive.

But here is the part that really matters. A bigger spleen could simply come from training. Muscles grow when you use them. Maybe spleens do too. So the team checked Bajau people who did not dive. And their spleens were just as large as those of the Bajau divers. There was no difference.

That points away from training and toward inheritance. The team then looked at Bajau DNA. They found signs of natural selection on a gene called PDE10A. In the study, this gene was linked to spleen size, possibly through thyroid hormone. They also found signs of selection on a gene called BDKRB2, which is linked to the diving response itself, the tightening of blood vessels. In other words, the sea may have shaped the Bajau body over many generations. People with a slightly better diving body brought home more food, and passed on their genes.

This was published in 2018 in the journal Cell. It became one of the clearest examples of recent human adaptation to a way of life. Not to a climate. Not to altitude. To a job.

And this is where we have to slow down. Because the story is great, but the evidence has limits.

First, this is one study, in one Bajau community, with fewer than one hundred people measured. It is a strong start, not the final word.

Second, the researchers themselves said almost nothing is known about the genetics of human spleen size. The gene link is a clue, not a finished explanation.

Third, a bigger spleen is only one piece. The Bajau also grow up in the water. In one village, children learned to swim at around three or four years old, simply by watching and copying. They learn how to move calmly, how to equalise, how to stay relaxed. That skill matters as much as any gene. So the honest picture is not "genes instead of training." It is genes and training, together, over a lifetime.

And fourth, the diving life has a cost. Ear damage is common among Bajau divers. Some people online claim they burst their eardrums on purpose. When a journalist asked, a Bajau diver said they do not do it on purpose. It happens. Their way of life is also changing. Many families have moved from boats to houses on stilts, and fewer children grow up diving every day.

So what can you take from the Bajau? You will not grow a Bajau spleen by watching this video. But the diving response is in you, and parts of it respond to training.

In one study, people did eight weeks of breath-hold training. Their resting spleen size went up, from about two hundred and forty to three hundred millilitres. Interesting. But the amount the spleen squeezed during a breath-hold did not change. So training changed something, but it did not turn them into Bajau.

Here is the coach's lesson.

One. Your body has systems you never use. The diving response is one of them. The Bajau show how far a human body can go when a life depends on it, and when that life is repeated for a thousand years.

Two. Genes set the ceiling. Practice decides how close you get to it. The Bajau have both. Most of us only control the second one, and that is enough to make real progress.

Three. And this one is not optional. Never practise breath-holding in water on your own. Not in a pool, not in the sea, not in a bath. When you hold your breath for long, you can lose consciousness without any warning. It is called shallow water blackout, and it has killed strong, fit swimmers. Breathing fast before you go under makes it more dangerous, not less. If you want to train your breath-hold, do it on dry land, sitting or lying down, and freedive only with a trained buddy watching you.

The Bajau did not become sea people in one lifetime. They became sea people over a thousand years. Respect the water the way they do.
