# Why Humans Are the Best Throwers on Earth
<!-- Pillar P4 · Human vs. · idea i068 · ~1,450 words · every claim has a line in sources.md -->

Pick up a ball and throw it as hard as you can. That simple move is something no other animal on Earth can really copy. An adult male chimpanzee, trained to throw, manages about twenty miles an hour. A twelve-year-old in Little League routinely throws sixty to seventy. A professional pitcher throws more than ninety.

Think about that for a second. The chimpanzee is one of our closest living relatives. Two arms. Two hands. A shoulder, an elbow, a wrist. The same basic body plan. And yet a child throws about three times faster.

Charles Darwin noticed this too. He thought that once our ancestors stood up on two legs, their hands were free. And a free hand could throw. Throwing let a hunter hurt an animal from a distance, without getting close to its horns or teeth.

But for a long time, nobody could explain how we became such good throwers. Or when. The answer came from a motion-capture lab at Harvard, a group of college baseball players, and some very fast cameras.

In 2013, a team led by Neil Roach, working with Daniel Lieberman, published a study in the journal Nature. They brought twenty young men into the lab. Sixteen of them were college baseball players. Markers on every joint. Cameras tracking every movement of every throw.

What they saw at the shoulder was extraordinary. Just before the ball leaves the hand, the upper arm spins inward. That spin can go faster than nine thousand degrees per second. If it could keep going, that would be twenty-five full turns in one second. The researchers called it the fastest motion the human body produces.

And here is the puzzle. Your shoulder muscles are not powerful enough to spin your arm that fast on their own. So where does the speed come from?

Think of a slingshot. You pull the band back slowly. It stores energy. And when you let go, the stone flies faster than your hand could ever push it. Your shoulder works in a similar way.

When you cock your arm back before a throw, the upper arm rotates far backwards. The ligaments, tendons and muscles across the front of the shoulder stretch, like that band. Then, in a few thousandths of a second, they snap back. In the team's model, this stored elastic energy could account for about half of the work that spins the arm forward in a typical throw.

To test the idea, they did something clever. They put braces on the throwers' shoulders. The braces limited how far the arm could cock back. Less stretch, less snap. And that is exactly what happened. The throwers lost about eight percent of their ball speed on average. And the work done at the shoulder during the cocking phase dropped by about forty percent.

So why can you do this, and a chimpanzee can't? The team pointed to three features of the human body.

First, a tall, mobile waist. Your hips and your chest can twist separately. That lets you rotate your hips first, then your torso, and whip that energy up into the arm.

Second, shoulders that sit low and face out to the sides. In an ape, the shoulder joint points more upward, which is great for climbing and hanging from branches. In you, the arm is lined up to swing.

Third, a twist in the upper arm bone itself. Throwing athletes keep a lower, more juvenile level of that twist into adulthood. That lets the arm rotate further back, and store more energy.

Now here is the part that makes this a story about evolution. According to the team, these three features first appear together in fossils from about two million years ago, in a species called Homo erectus. And that is around the time the archaeological record suggests hunting was becoming more important. Their conclusion: throwing to hunt probably played an important role in the evolution of our genus, Homo.

So when you throw a ball in the park, you may be using a design that is about two million years old. Your body carries a very long training log. Throwing could be one of its oldest entries.

But here is where the story gets complicated.

First, bones tell us what a body could do. They do not tell us what it actually did. The oldest throwing weapons ever found are wooden spears from Schöningen, in Germany. They were dug up in the 1990s. For years, they were described as about three hundred thousand years old. In 2025, a new dating study put them at around two hundred thousand. And by then, the likely makers were Neanderthals, not Homo erectus.

That leaves a gap of well over a million years between the first throwing shoulder and the first surviving spear. Wood rots. A thrown stone looks like any other stone. So the early chapters of this story are read from anatomy, not from weapons.

Could those spears really be thrown at an animal? In 2019, archaeologist Annemieke Milks and her team made replicas, weighing about seven hundred and sixty and eight hundred grams. They gave them to six trained javelin athletes. The athletes hit targets at up to twenty metres. That was about double the distance scientists had previously thought possible. But remember who was throwing. Trained modern athletes, not ancient hunters.

The second complication matters for you directly. The same snap that makes the throw so fast puts enormous stress on the joint. Roach and his team wrote it plainly. The ligaments and tendons of the human shoulder and elbow are not well adapted to withstand that repeated stretching. And they frequently suffer from laxity and tearing.

Other researchers measured the loads in skilled adult pitchers. Just before the arm reaches full cock, the elbow has to resist a twisting load of about sixty-four newton-metres. Pitch after pitch.

And our ancestors probably did not throw the way a modern pitcher does. A hunter might make a few important throws in a day. A professional pitcher can easily throw more than a hundred very fast pitches in two or three hours. The design is old. The workload is new.

So here is my lesson, as a strength coach.

Your throw does not start in your arm. It starts at the ground. It travels up through your legs, your hips and your waist, and only then does it whip through the shoulder. That is how the body is built. So train it that way. Strong legs and hips. A trunk that can rotate with control. And a shoulder that you prepare, not just use.

Second, respect the snap. The stretch that powers your throw is the same stretch that loads the tissue. If you, or your kids, play a throwing sport, build up the number of hard throws gradually. Warm up before you throw hard. And do not go from zero to full effort after weeks of rest.

Third, a tired arm is a warning. Youth baseball safety guidelines put pitching while tired at the top of their list of injury risks. They set daily pitch limits and rest days by age. And they recommend at least two months a year with no overhead sport at all. Three to four months is better.

And if your shoulder or elbow hurts when you throw, do not push through it. Get it checked.

You carry a two-million-year-old slingshot on each side of your body. It is one of the best designs evolution ever made. Just remember, it was not built for a hundred fastballs a night. I am a strength and conditioning coach, and this is Homo Athleticus.
