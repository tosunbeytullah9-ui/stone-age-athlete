# Why Ancient Humans Were Tougher Than Today's Athletes (And Where They Weren't)

Right now, somewhere, an elite athlete is finishing a two-hour training session. They lifted, they sprinted, they pushed to the edge. And in an hour, they'll be sitting. On a couch, in a car, at a desk. For the next ten hours.

Now rewind seven thousand years. A woman kneels over a flat stone. In her hands, a heavier stone. She pushes it forward, pulls it back, grinding grain into flour. Possibly for up to five hours. Day after day.

When scientists scanned arm bones like hers, they found something that should not be possible. Her arm bones were stronger than those of today's elite women rowers. Not a little stronger. Eleven to sixteen percent stronger. And compared to ordinary students, almost thirty percent.

Those rowers were training twice a day, covering about a hundred and twenty kilometers a week. And she never trained for sport at all. So how is that possible? And does it mean our ancestors were better athletes than we are?

The answer is more surprising than a simple yes. Because the same bones that make our ancestors look superhuman also reveal exactly where they fell short. Let's start with what they got right.
