# You Don't Need 8 Glasses of Water a Day

Eight glasses of water a day. You have heard it a thousand times. And it started with a sentence that nobody finished reading.

Case file zero zero one. Subject: you. Belief: you must drink eight glasses of water a day, or your body slowly turns into a raisin.

I am Mortimer. I work at the Bureau of Human Error. My job is to file everything humanity gets wrong. I have been doing it for about three thousand years. The archive is not getting smaller.

Now, I am not judging. I believed this one too. I carried a water bottle for most of the nineteen-nineties. It was very heavy. It did nothing for my personality.

So let's open the file, and see who wrote the original report.

The year is nineteen forty-five. In the United States, a government panel called the Food and Nutrition Board publishes its advice on water. An adult, it says, needs about two and a half liters a day. Roughly one milliliter for every calorie of food.

That is about ten glasses. So far, the legend looks almost modest. [pause] But the board did not stop there. The very next sentence says, and I quote: "Most of this quantity is contained in prepared foods."

Most of it. Is already. In your food. That sentence did not make it into the legend. Somewhere between nineteen forty-five and your gym bag, humanity read the first half, nodded, and went to fill a bottle.

Then, in the nineteen-seventies, a well-known American nutritionist, Frederick Stare, recommended at least six glasses a day. He never explained where the number came from. Over the years, six became eight. A suggestion became a commandment. And the commandment got its own bottle.

In two thousand and two, a kidney specialist named Heinz Valtin decided to check. He was a professor at Dartmouth Medical School, and he had written textbooks on how the body balances water. He went looking for the science behind eight glasses a day. He found no scientific proof that every person must drink that much.

Instead, he pointed at the obvious. You get lots of water from solid food. Especially fruit and vegetables. Even bread is roughly a third water. You have been drinking your sandwich for years.

And it is not only food. Since two thousand and four, the official American guideline has counted total water, from all foods and all drinks. About three point seven liters a day for men, and two point seven for women.

Now here is the number that ruins the rule. When American researchers looked at what adults actually consumed, plain drinking water was only about a third of it. Thirty percent for men. Thirty-four percent for women. The rest came from other drinks, and from food.

"But coffee dehydrates you." I know. That one has its own drawer. In two thousand and fourteen, researchers in Britain gave fifty men, all daily coffee drinkers, four cups of coffee a day for three days. Then the same amount of water, for three days. Their hydration looked the same. Moderate coffee, in people used to it, hydrated about as well as water.

While that drawer is open, here is its neighbour. "If you feel thirsty, you are already dehydrated." You have heard that one too. Usually from someone holding a very large bottle.

The earliest version in our files comes from an Illinois newspaper, in nineteen eighty-four. A fitness expert said it about people exercising for more than an hour, in summer heat. Sensible advice. Then the details fell off. The summer went missing. The hour went missing. The exercise went missing. What was left was a sentence about everybody, all the time, including people sitting on a sofa.

Valtin checked this one as well. Thirst switches on when your blood has become less than two percent more concentrated. Most experts would say dehydration begins at around five percent. So in daily life, thirst is not a late alarm. It is an early one. Your body is not that badly designed. I have seen worse. I have seen the appendix.

There is one exception, and it is the original one. Long, hard exercise in the heat. That is where sports scientists trust a bathroom scale more than a feeling. Hold that thought. Our coach will come back to it.

Filing note. Subscribing to this channel is free. I checked. I have had three thousand years.

So how much water does a body actually move through in a day? For a long time, nobody had measured it in large numbers. In two thousand and twenty-two, a large international team published the answer in the journal Science. They had data from five thousand six hundred and four people, in twenty-six countries, aged eight days to ninety-six years.

Each person drank a small, measured dose of specially labeled water. Harmless isotopes that can be traced. Then the scientists followed how fast that water moved through the body. Not what people said they drank. What their bodies actually did.

The result? Daily water turnover ranged from about one liter to about six. Some bodies moved six times as much water as others. One number for everybody was never going to fit.

The biggest reasons were physical activity, and being an athlete. Then sex, the kind of country you live in, and age. Heat and humidity mattered too. All else being equal, an athlete turned over about one liter more per day.

And the authors wrote it down, plainly. The common suggestion to drink eight glasses a day is not backed by objective evidence.

My favourite detail is this. About a tenth of the water passing through you is made by your own body, when it burns food. You are, in a small way, a water factory. Nobody prints that on the bottle.

[serious] But here is where the file gets complicated. Because "eight glasses is wrong" does not mean "water does not matter."

First, look at the numbers again. For a typical twenty-year-old man in a mild climate, the study predicted about three point two liters of turnover a day. For a woman, about two point seven. That is more than eight glasses. It is just that a big part of it never comes out of a glass.

Second, some people really do need more. Athletes. People doing hard physical work. Anyone in serious heat. And anyone whose doctor has given them their own rules.

Third, and this is the part the bottle never mentions, you can drink too much. In two thousand and two, doctors took blood from four hundred and eighty-eight runners at the finish of the Boston Marathon. About thirteen percent had abnormally low sodium in their blood. A condition called hyponatremia. A small number were in the critical range.

The strongest warning sign was weight gain during the race. Those runners had drunk more than they had sweated out. They followed "drink as much as you can" a little too faithfully.

So, back to the file. Eight glasses of water a day. A board in nineteen forty-five named an amount, and in the very next sentence said most of it is already in your food. A nutritionist turned it into a habit. And the habit outlived the footnote.

Verdict: wrong. [pause] Not dangerous for most people. Not useless. Just wrong. Your water comes from your food, from every drink you have, and even from your own body. The glass is only part of the story.

Now, a filing note from the strength and conditioning coach who checks our body files. Practical, and safe.

One. If you are a healthy adult in normal conditions, thirst is a good guide. Physiologists call thirst the main driver of how much we drink. Drink when you are thirsty, drink with meals, and drink a little more when it is hot.

Two. If you train hard, stop guessing. Weigh yourself before and after a session, without the sweaty clothes. The difference is mostly sweat. The American College of Sports Medicine advises athletes not to lose more than about two percent of their body weight to water loss during exercise.

Three. In long events, do not drink so much that you gain weight. Gaining weight during a marathon is a warning sign. Not an achievement.

And four. If you are older, pregnant, ill, on medication, or you have kidney or heart problems, your needs are different. Ask your doctor. Not a water bottle.

Case closed. Your file, however, remains open. Next case: a costume designer, an opera house, and a helmet that never existed.
