# You Can't Burn Belly Fat With Crunches
<!-- You're Wrong! — Case file 005 — Body Files (Tuesday) -->

Somewhere, right now, someone is doing their three hundredth crunch. Teeth clenched. Stomach burning. Absolutely convinced that the fat on their belly is melting, right there, under the burn.

It is a lovely image. Work the muscle, and the fat sitting on top of it simply gives up and leaves. Crunches for the belly. Leg raises for the thighs. Little arm kickbacks for the wobbly bit under the arm.

Case file zero zero five. Subject: you, and your stomach. Belief: work a muscle hard enough, and the fat on top of it burns away.

The idea has a name, you see. Spot reduction. The belief that you can choose where your body loses fat, by exercising the muscle underneath it. And an entire industry has been very happy for you to believe it.

In April nineteen ninety-one, a little hinged gadget appeared on American television. You put it between your knees, and you squeezed. It promised better thighs. It cost about twenty dollars. Six million were sold in eighteen months, and total sales eventually passed three hundred million dollars.

A decade later came the electric belts. Strap one around your waist, let it buzz your stomach muscles, and watch the inches vanish. One advert promised you would lose four inches in thirty days. Another claimed that ten minutes equalled six hundred sit-ups.

In May two thousand two, the United States Federal Trade Commission took three of the best-selling belts to court for false claims. Its chairman said, and I have this one framed, that there are no magic pills, potions or pulsators for losing weight.

[pause] But the gadgets were only selling a story. Let us look at the evidence. Five exhibits. I have arranged them in order of increasing cruelty.

Exhibit one. Tennis players. Back in nineteen seventy-one, a researcher found the perfect natural experiment. A serious tennis player uses one arm far more than the other, for years. So he measured twenty of them.

The playing arm was bigger. More muscle, thicker forearm, thicker upper arm. And the fat over those muscles? The same thickness on both sides. Years of work on one arm, and the fat did not notice.

Exhibit two. The sit-up study. In nineteen eighty-four, researchers took nineteen people and split them into two groups. One group did sit-ups. Every day. For twenty-seven days. By the end, each of them had done five thousand and four sit-ups.

Then the researchers took tiny samples of fat. From the belly, from the upper back, and from the buttocks. The fat cells had shrunk a little. But they had shrunk by about the same amount everywhere. The belly got no special treatment. Five thousand sit-ups, and the bottom benefited just as much.

Exhibit three. In two thousand eleven, a study put twenty-four sedentary adults on an abdominal programme. Seven exercises, two sets of ten, five days a week, for six weeks. A control group did nothing at all.

After six weeks, the exercise group could do far more curl-ups. Forty-seven on average, against thirty-two for the do-nothings. Their stomach muscles had clearly improved. And their belly fat, their waist size and their body fat percentage? No significant change. Stronger abs. Same belly.

Exhibit four. A much bigger study, in two thousand seven. One hundred and four people trained one arm for twelve weeks, and left the other arm alone. Then their arms went into an MRI scanner.

There was no meaningful difference in fat loss between the trained arm and the lazy one. Fat had come off, a little, all over. The arm that did all the work got no reward for it.

[pause] And now exhibit five. My favourite. In two thousand thirteen, eleven sports science students spent twelve weeks training one leg. Only one leg. On a leg press, with light weights, for about a thousand repetitions per session. Each session took around eighty minutes. Of one leg.

At the end, they had lost fat. Not much, about seven hundred grams. But here is the cruel part. The fat did not come off the trained leg. It came off the trunk and the arms. Twelve weeks of punishing one leg, and the body chose to slim down the arms.

Exhibit five, part two. In two thousand twenty-one, a team pooled every well-controlled study they could find that compared a trained limb with an untrained one. Thirteen studies. One thousand, one hundred and fifty-eight people. Thirty-seven comparisons.

Seventeen comparisons favoured the trained side. Twenty favoured the untrained side. The overall effect was, statistically speaking, zero. In the authors' own words, spot reduction was not observed.

So why does it feel so true? Because of the burn. When you do crunch after crunch, your stomach really does burn. It feels like something is being melted. It is not. That burning is the muscle working hard, not fat leaving. And if you are wondering what causes the burn, we covered that in case file zero zero three. Short version: it is not what you think either.

So why does the belly ignore the belly exercise? The answer is in how fat actually works.

Fat is stored in fat cells as triglycerides. When your body needs energy, a muscle does not reach into the nearest fat cell and help itself. Instead, hormones such as adrenaline travel through the blood and signal fat cells all over the body. Those cells break their fat down into fatty acids, and release them into the bloodstream. From there, the fuel goes wherever it is needed.

Think of it like this. Your fat is not a wallet in each pocket. It is one central bank. When your legs need money, the bank does not empty the wallet nearest your legs. It sends a transfer from wherever it likes.

And even then, the fat cells do not disappear. In adults, the number of fat cells stays remarkably steady. When you lose fat, the cells mostly shrink, like balloons letting out air. So you are not deleting the belly. You are deflating it. Slowly. Along with everything else.

So which part of the bank pays out first? Mostly, you do not get a vote. Where your body stores fat, and which stores it gives up first, depends on your genes, your sex, your age and your hormones. Men tend to store more around the abdomen. Women, before menopause, tend to carry more on the hips and thighs. Fat in the upper body is also more active in releasing its stores than fat in the lower body. None of this asks for your opinion.

[serious] Now, in the interest of fairness, the Bureau notes one complication. A small Danish study had ten men exercise one leg at a time. The fat sitting right next to the working muscle did get a little more blood flow, and released its fat a little faster than the fat next to the resting leg.

So there may be a tiny local effect. But tiny is the important word. When researchers look at what actually changes in the mirror, across thousands of people, it does not show up. A whisper of a signal, drowned out by the whole body shouting.

Which brings us to the six-pack. Here is a secret the belt adverts would rather you did not hear. You already have one. Almost everyone does. The muscle is there. Whether anyone can see it depends on the layer of fat covering it.

Crunches can make the muscle stronger. They cannot remove the curtain. And you cannot pull back a curtain from just one corner of the room.

An expert strength and conditioning coach, who works with elite athletes, puts it simply. Train your core for what it actually does. It braces your spine, and it transfers force between your legs and your arms. That is worth doing for its own sake. But if the goal is less fat, the answer is the whole body. Big lifts, hard conditioning, enough sleep, and an honest look at what is on your plate.

Lose fat overall, and it will come off everywhere, in an order that your genes decide. Including, eventually, the bit you were crunching at.

[pause] Verdict: wrong.

You cannot choose where you lose fat. Exercising a muscle builds that muscle. It does not drain the fat on top of it. The tennis players proved it, the sit-ups proved it, and the poor one-legged students proved it most of all.

You can choose where you build muscle. That part is yours. Where the fat leaves from is a decision your body made long before you bought a belt.

The gadget makers have known this for decades. It has not stopped them. It has not stopped you, either. I have the receipts.

Case closed. Do one crunch for me. Just the one. Strictly for morale. Next case: a thumb, a painting, and a gladiator who lost four times and walked out every time.
