# Coca-Cola Didn't Make Santa Red
<!-- You're Wrong! — Case file 010 — History Files (Friday) -->

Every December, someone at a party leans in with a fact. Did you know that Santa Claus used to wear green? Then Coca-Cola dressed him in red, to match the brand. The jolly fat man in the red suit is basically an advertisement.

It is a perfect party fact. It is surprising, it is a little cynical, and it makes the person telling it sound like they have seen behind the curtain. It is also, for the most part, wrong.

Case file zero one zero. Subject: you, and everyone at that party. Belief: Coca-Cola invented the red Santa Claus.

I have spent a great many Christmases in the archive. It is quiet, there is no tinsel, and nobody asks me to wear a paper hat. So I have had plenty of time to look at old pictures of Santa. Hundreds of them. He rarely looks surprised to see me. Let us look at a few together.

[pause] First, where does Santa come from? His roots go back to Saint Nicholas, a fourth-century bishop in what is now Turkey, famous for secret gift-giving. In European art, Saint Nicholas is usually shown as a bearded bishop in church robes. The Dutch brought their Sinterklaas to New York, and over time he became the American Santa Claus.

The modern version really took shape in eighteen twenty-three, with a poem published in New York. A Visit from Saint Nicholas. You know it as The Night Before Christmas. It gave us the sleigh, the reindeer, the chimney and the sack of toys. And it described Santa as chubby and plump, a right jolly old elf.

An elf. With a miniature sleigh and tiny reindeer. The original modern Santa was small enough to fit down a chimney without a struggle. The poem says nothing at all about the colour of his clothes.

It did, however, name the reindeer. Dasher, Dancer, Prancer, Vixen, Comet, Cupid, and two called Dunder and Blixem, Dutch for thunder and lightning. Those last two were later tidied up into Donner and Blitzen. Rudolph would not arrive for over a century. He was, as it happens, created for a department store promotion. But that is another file.

Exhibit one. Thomas Nast. A political cartoonist for Harper's Weekly, the same Nast who helped popularise the elephant as the symbol of the Republican Party. On the third of January, eighteen sixty-three, in the middle of the American Civil War, Nast drew Santa visiting a Union army camp. That Santa wore stars and stripes. He was, quite literally, dressed as the flag. In one hand, he dangles a puppet that looks suspiciously like the Confederate president, Jefferson Davis. Santa had picked a side.

Nast went on to draw Santa for more than twenty years. Thirty-three illustrations. In eighteen sixty-six, he gave him a workshop, and an address. Santa Claussville, North Pole. And in eighteen eighty-one, he published Merry Old Santa Claus. A round, white-bearded, cheerful man with a pipe, an armful of toys, and a red suit trimmed with white fur.

Eighteen eighty-one. Coca-Cola was invented in eighteen eighty-six. Santa was in red five years before the drink existed.

Exhibit two. Puck. Puck was a popular American humour magazine, printed in full colour. On its Christmas covers in nineteen oh two, nineteen oh four and nineteen oh five, Santa appears in red and white, essentially in the form you know today. Big, bearded, jolly. More than a quarter of a century before Coca-Cola's famous campaign.

By the nineteen twenties, the image had become so standard that in nineteen twenty-seven, a New York Times article noted that a standardised Santa Claus appears to New York children. Four years before Coca-Cola painted a single one.

[pause] Exhibit three. Coca-Cola was not even the first soft drink to use him. That honour goes to a company called White Rock Beverages, which sold mineral water and ginger ale. White Rock used Santa to sell mineral water in nineteen fifteen, and again in colour in its ginger ale advertisements from nineteen twenty-three. Santa was selling fizzy drinks long before he was selling Coke. And Coca-Cola was not the last, either. Pepsi used very similar Santa paintings in the nineteen forties and fifties. Nobody remembers that, which tells you something about who had the better artist.

So where did the green Santa come from? Here, the party fact is not entirely made up. It just has the wrong address.

In Britain, there was a separate character called Father Christmas. Older, more of a symbol of feasting and good cheer than a delivery service. His costume varied a great deal. In early pictures, he appears in brown, green, blue or white robes, although even then, red was the most common colour. In eighteen fifty-four, one story describes him in a long brown robe, spotted white with snow. In eighteen eighty-one, a children's poem asks whether Father Christmas will come robed in green, with beard all white.

The most famous green one comes from Charles Dickens. In A Christmas Carol, in eighteen forty-three, the Ghost of Christmas Present appears to Scrooge. In John Leech's illustration, he is a large, jolly, bearded figure with a holly wreath, surrounded by food, wearing a loose green robe trimmed with fur. Dickens never calls him Father Christmas. But he looks very much like the British idea of Christmas in person.

Over the nineteenth century, the British Father Christmas and the American Santa Claus gradually merged. By the early twentieth century, children on both sides of the Atlantic were imagining the same man, in more or less the same clothes. A report from nineteen fifteen noted that the two had effected a happy compromise. Even so, as late as nineteen eighty-five, the Illustrated London News observed that the British still preferred their own long-robed, big-bearded Father Christmas. So yes, there were green Christmas figures. They simply faded out long before Coca-Cola came along.

And here is my favourite twist. In Brazil, in the two thousands, a soft drink company called Dolly ran Christmas commercials with Santa dressed in green. The green Santa caught on. So there really is a soft drink that changed the colour of Santa's suit. It just was not Coca-Cola, and it made him green.

[serious] Now, to be fair to Coca-Cola. Because the company did do something real.

In nineteen thirty-one, Coca-Cola hired an illustrator named Haddon Sundblom to paint Santa for its Christmas advertising. Sundblom went back to the old poem for inspiration, and painted Santa as warm, friendly, pleasantly plump and human. Not a small elf. Not a stern bishop. A big, rosy grandfather you would happily leave a snack for.

His first model was a friend of his, named Lou Prentice. And Sundblom kept painting Santa for Coca-Cola for over thirty years, until nineteen sixty-four. Those paintings appeared in magazines, on posters and on shop displays, again and again, year after year, across America and then much of the world.

That kind of repetition is incredibly powerful. Coca-Cola did not invent the red Santa. But it may well have done more than anyone else to fix one particular version of him in the global imagination. The rosy cheeks, the twinkle, the round belly, the cosy warmth. When you picture Santa today, there is a decent chance you are picturing a Sundblom.

And there is a reason the myth persists. Coca-Cola's colours happen to be red and white. So are Santa's. It is a coincidence that makes a very convincing story. The suit matches the can. Surely that is not an accident? It is. The suit came first. The can simply had good taste.

Verdict: mostly wrong.

Coca-Cola did not make Santa red. Thomas Nast had him in red by eighteen eighty-one, before the drink was invented. Puck magazine had him in red and white at the start of the twentieth century. Another soft drink company used him in its adverts first. And the famous green figure was a different British tradition that had already faded away.

What Coca-Cola did was paint the most popular version of Santa, beautifully, for over three decades, and put it in front of the whole world. It did not create him. It polished him.

The real lesson is about how myths survive. A story that is surprising, slightly cynical, and makes the teller sound clever will travel further than a dull truth. Even when the dull truth is printed on a magazine cover in nineteen oh two.

I have filed this one under advertising, cross-referenced with coincidences.
