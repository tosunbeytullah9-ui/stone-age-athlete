# Lactic Acid Isn't Why You're Sore
<!-- Case 003 · Body Files · idea i003 · verdict WRONG · narrator: Mortimer · target ~1,550 words ≈ 9 min at ~180 wpm
     Body file: the coach checks it. Every number is in claims.yaml. No jokes about weight or bodies. -->

Two days after leg day, the stairs become your enemy. And you know exactly who to blame. Lactic acid. [pause] Here is the problem. Lactic acid had left your body before you even got home.

Case file zero zero three. Subject: you. Belief: lactic acid builds up in your muscles and makes them sore for days.

I believed this one for most of the twentieth century. Which, for me, is a long weekend. I even told people to stretch the lactic acid out. With confidence. In a lab coat I was not technically entitled to wear.

So let's open the file, and see who wrote the original report.

The year is nineteen oh seven. At the University of Cambridge, two scientists, Walter Fletcher and Frederick Gowland Hopkins, are studying frog muscles. They make the muscles work without oxygen, and they find something new. The working muscle produces lactic acid. And when oxygen comes back, the lactic acid disappears.

It was brilliant work. Hopkins later won a Nobel Prize, for something else entirely. But this experiment started a whole new science of muscle chemistry. And it planted an idea: muscles without enough oxygen make lactic acid, and lactic acid is the price you pay.

Then came the British physiologist Archibald Vivian Hill. In nineteen twenty-two, he shared the Nobel Prize with the German biochemist Otto Meyerhof, for discoveries about what happens inside a contracting muscle. Hill described an "oxygen debt" that the body pays back after hard exercise. In his picture, the extra oxygen you breathe during recovery burns about a fifth of the lactic acid you made, and the energy from that rebuilds the rest back into muscle fuel. An elegant idea. And it made lactic acid the main character of exhaustion. Now the molecule had a Nobel Prize next to its name. And a reputation.

For most of the century, lactic acid was treated as the villain of exercise. The waste product. The reason you slow down. The burning in your legs. And, eventually, the soreness two days later. [pause] That last part, nobody really tested. It just sounded like the same villain.

So let's test it. Starting with the timing.

When you exercise hard, lactate in your blood goes up. That part is true. But after you stop, it comes back down fast. Researchers have shown it returns to resting levels within about an hour.

Now look at the soreness. According to a review by the exercise scientists Brad Schoenfeld and Bret Contreras, it usually becomes noticeable six to eight hours after a hard session, and peaks at around forty-eight hours. Two days.

So the suspect left the building about an hour after the crime, and the victim only noticed the damage the next day. As a filing clerk, I would like to point out that this is not how evidence works.

Second piece of evidence: running downhill. In nineteen eighty-three, a team led by the physiologist James Schwane compared running on the level with running downhill. Level running pushed lactate up. Downhill running did not. And yet downhill running is the one famous for leaving people sore for days.

Why? Because running downhill is mostly braking. Your thigh muscles work while they are being stretched, to stop you from falling forward. That kind of lengthening work, called eccentric work, is the kind most likely to leave you sore. Very little lactate. A lot of soreness. The two do not even turn up to the same appointments.

Third piece of evidence: the name itself. The exercise physiologist Robert Robergs has spent years pointing out that your cells actually produce lactate, not lactic acid. And the chemistry that makes lactate does not create the acid problem. If anything, it works against it.

And fourth, the most embarrassing part for the old story. Lactate is not waste. It is fuel. At the University of California, Berkeley, the physiologist George Brooks spent decades showing that lactate moves from cell to cell around the body, in what he called the lactate shuttle. His work found that the heart and the brain run more efficiently on lactate than on glucose. Your muscles burn it too, especially during hard effort.

Brooks has a neat way of putting it. Lactate production, he says, is a strain response. It is there to compensate for metabolic stress. In other words, lactate shows up because you are working hard. It is the smoke alarm, not the fire. And for about a hundred years, we kept arresting the smoke alarm.

So we blamed the soreness on the molecule that was busy delivering lunch. If you have ever paid someone to massage the lactic acid out of your legs two days after a race, I am afraid you paid them to chase a molecule that had already left. The massage may still have helped. We will come back to that. It just was not evicting anyone.

So if it is not lactic acid, what is it? The current explanation is less dramatic and more physical. When you do more than your muscles are used to, especially that braking, eccentric kind of work, you create small-scale damage in the muscle fibres. Over the next day or two, your body cleans up and repairs. That repair process is what you feel.

And here is the good news. Muscles adapt to this remarkably fast. If you repeat the same activity within about two weeks, the damage and the soreness are usually much smaller. Your first session protects you from the second. It is one of the few times in life when suffering early is actually a good investment.

[serious] But here is where the file gets complicated. Because there are two different pains here, and they are not the same thing.

The first is the soreness that arrives a day or two later. That is the one lactic acid has been falsely accused of. The second is the burning you feel during a hard set, while you are still doing it. And that burning has a more interesting story.

In twenty fourteen, researchers in the United States injected tiny amounts of exercise chemicals into the thumb muscle of ten volunteers. Lactate on its own did nothing. Acid on its own did nothing. Even the energy molecule ATP on its own did nothing. But together, at the levels you would find during hard exercise, they produced feelings of fatigue, and at higher levels, an ache.

So lactate is not innocent in every case. It looks like one member of a small gang that helps create the burn during exercise. Just never the lone villain. And not the one who sneaks back two days later. One researcher has even argued that lactate's role in delayed soreness should be looked at again through the nerves. That is a hypothesis, and an interesting one. It is not the old story.

While the file is open, one more correction. Many people treat soreness as proof of a good workout. No pain, no gain. Schoenfeld and Contreras looked at this too. Their conclusion: soreness is a poor way to judge a training session, and it should not be used as a measure of results. You can get stronger without being sore. And you can be very sore after a session that did very little for you, like your first time trying a new sport at a wedding.

So, back to the file. Lactic acid makes you sore for days. The lactate is gone in about an hour. The soreness peaks around two days later. The exercise that makes the most soreness makes the least lactate. And the molecule itself is a fuel your heart and brain are happy to use.

Verdict: wrong. [pause] Wrong molecule. Wrong timing. And technically, wrong name.

Now, a filing note from the strength and conditioning coach who checks our body files. Practical, and safe.

One. Soreness is not the scoreboard. Track what actually matters: the weight you lift, the reps you do, how well you move, how you feel over weeks. If those go up, the training works, sore or not.

Two. When you start something new, or come back after a break, start lighter than your ego wants. Especially with downhill running, jumping, and slow lowering movements. Your first session is a vaccine for the second. Give it a week or two before you go all out.

Three. If you are sore anyway, you do not need a magic potion. In a twenty eighteen review of ninety-nine studies, massage was the most effective method for reducing soreness and feelings of fatigue. Light active recovery, compression clothing and cold or contrast water helped a little. Stretching, sadly, did not.

And four. Normal soreness is dull, spread across the muscle, and fades within about a week. If the pain is sharp, sits in a joint, or keeps getting worse instead of better, that is not soreness. Get it checked.
