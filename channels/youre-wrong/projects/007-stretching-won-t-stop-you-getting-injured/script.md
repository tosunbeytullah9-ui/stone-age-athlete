# Stretching Won't Stop You Getting Injured
<!-- You're Wrong! — Case file 007 — Body Files (Tuesday) -->

Picture a school sports field, any time in the last fifty years. Before anyone is allowed to run, the teacher blows a whistle. Everyone bends over and touches their toes. Hold it. Count to twenty. Now the other leg.

Why? Because everybody knows that if you do not stretch before exercise, you will pull something. Stretching warms up the muscles. Stretching prevents injuries. Your teacher said so. Your coach said so. Your gym's poster says so, next to a drawing of a very flexible man in a headband.

Case file zero zero seven. Subject: you, and every PE teacher you ever had. Belief: stretching before exercise stops you getting injured.

Let me be clear from the start, before the yoga community sends a delegation. Stretching is not useless. It is very good at one thing. That thing is range of motion. Stretch regularly, over weeks and months, and you will be able to move further. Even a single session increases your range for a while, usually for less than half an hour. The question today is whether it does the thing everyone uses it for. Preventing injuries, just before you exercise.

First, a little history. Stretching became a ritual during the fitness boom of the nineteen seventies. In nineteen seventy-five, an American couple self-published a small book called Stretching. It was an immediate hit. It went on to sell more than three and a half million copies, in twenty-nine foreign editions. Runners stretched. Office workers stretched. Hollywood stretched. I stretched once, in nineteen seventy-six. I pulled a filing cabinet over.

And nobody had really tested whether a stretch before exercise actually stopped injuries. It simply seemed obvious. In science, that is called an untested assumption. In sport, it was called tradition. Which, at the Bureau, is usually where the trouble starts.

[pause] So, the evidence. Exhibit one is the army.

In the late nineteen nineties, researchers in Australia had a perfect laboratory. Army recruits. Young men, all doing the same punishing training, at the same time, on the same schedule. One thousand, five hundred and thirty-eight of them were randomly split into two groups.

One group stretched before every training session. Under supervision. One twenty-second stretch for each of six major leg muscle groups, every single warm-up. The other group did the same warm-up, without the stretching.

Twelve weeks later, the researchers counted the injuries. Three hundred and thirty-three lower-limb injuries in total. The stretching group had one hundred and fifty-eight. The group that did not stretch had one hundred and seventy-five.

That sounds like a small win, until you account for everything else. Statistically, the difference was nothing. The authors concluded that a typical pre-exercise stretching routine does not produce a meaningful reduction in the risk of injury. Instead, they pointed to something else worth working on. Fitness.

Exhibit two. In two thousand fourteen, a team in Denmark pooled the best evidence they could find on preventing sports injuries. Twenty-five randomised trials. Twenty-six thousand, six hundred and ten people. Almost three and a half thousand injuries.

They compared different strategies. Balance training helped. Programmes that combined several methods helped. Stretching did not. Its effect was, statistically, a shrug.

And the winner, by a long way? Strength training. Programmes that made people stronger cut sports injuries to less than one third. Overuse injuries were almost halved. The exercise that everybody skips was the one that actually worked. The ritual that everybody does was the one that did not.

One example. The hamstring strain. The classic I pulled something injury, exactly the thing the toe-touching was supposed to prevent. There is a brutal little strength exercise called the Nordic hamstring curl. You kneel, someone holds your ankles, and you lower yourself slowly towards the floor. A two thousand nineteen review of fifteen studies, with eight thousand four hundred and fifty-nine athletes, found that programmes including it cut hamstring injuries by up to fifty-one per cent. Strength, not stretch.

[pause] Exhibit three. It gets worse. Some studies found that static stretching, just before you need to perform, can make you slightly weaker.

In two thousand thirteen, a review combined more than a hundred studies. On average, a static stretch before exercise reduced maximal strength by around five per cent. Explosive performance, jumping and sprinting, dropped by about two per cent.

Now, before anyone panics, the details matter. The longer the stretch, the bigger the drop. A short stretch, under a minute per muscle, has only a tiny effect. And if you follow your stretching with some dynamic movement, most of that loss disappears. So a quick stretch in your warm-up will not ruin your session. And dynamic stretching, swinging and moving your limbs through their range, actually tends to help a little. On average, performance goes up by around one per cent.

But think about the logic for a moment. The traditional pre-match ritual was a long, static stretch, held patiently, right before sprinting. Which turns out to be the one version most likely to make you slower, without making you safer. It is rather like polishing your car by letting the air out of the tyres.

Exhibit four. Soreness. Surely stretching at least stops the aches the next day?

In two thousand eleven, the Cochrane Collaboration, which reviews medical evidence for a living, looked at exactly this. Twelve studies, including one large trial with more than two thousand people. Stretching before exercise, after exercise, or both, reduced soreness by about one point. On a one-hundred-point scale.

One point. You would not notice it. Even in the big trial, the difference in peak soreness over a week was under four points out of a hundred. Real, measurable, and completely irrelevant to your stairs the next morning. The reviewers concluded that stretching does not produce clinically important reductions in muscle soreness. And if you want to know what soreness actually is, case file zero zero three has the paperwork.

[serious] So why does stretching feel like it helps? A few reasons.

First, it feels good. A stretch genuinely makes you feel looser. But research suggests a lot of that new flexibility comes from your nervous system tolerating the stretch better, rather than the muscle itself suddenly changing. You have not rebuilt the muscle. You have negotiated with it.

Second, it is part of a warm-up. And warming up genuinely matters. Raising your body temperature, getting blood moving, rehearsing the movements you are about to do. When people stretched before sport and did not get hurt, they credited the stretching. The warm-up quietly did the work, and the stretch took the bow.

Third, it is a ritual. Before a big moment, people like to do something familiar with their hands and their nerves. Touching your toes is calming. The Bureau has nothing against calm. We only object when calm is filed under injury prevention.

And when you look at warm-ups that are designed properly, the results are impressive. In two thousand eight, researchers in Norway tested a twenty-minute football warm-up on almost nineteen hundred teenage girls. Running, balance, jumping, and strength exercises like planks and hamstring lowers. Over one season, overall injuries fell by about a third. Severe injuries fell by almost half. Versions of that programme are now used by football teams around the world, under the name the eleven plus.

Note what was in that warm-up. Movement, balance and strength. Not twenty seconds of touching your toes.

An expert strength and conditioning coach, who works with elite gymnasts, has an interesting view on this. In gymnastics, flexibility is not optional. Athletes need extreme range of motion, and stretching is part of how they build it, over months and years. But that work is planned as training, with its own time and purpose. It is not a magic shield you throw on in the last five minutes before a hard session.

Before the hard work, the coach wants the body warm, moving and switched on. Pulses up. Joints moving through the range they will use. A few rehearsal reps of the actual skill. Then, if an athlete needs a particular range, a short stretch, followed by more movement. Long, slow holds belong to a different part of the day.

[pause] Verdict: wrong.

A static stretch before exercise does not meaningfully prevent injury. In an army of recruits, it changed nothing. In twenty-five trials, it changed nothing. Held for too long, right before you sprint, it can even make you a little slower and weaker. And it does next to nothing for soreness.

What does protect you is less glamorous. Being fit. Being strong. And a real warm-up that gets your body moving the way the sport will move it.

Stretching is still worth doing, if you need more range of motion. Just stop treating it as armour.
