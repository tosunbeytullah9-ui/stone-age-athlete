# Ancient People Didn't Die at 30
<!-- You're Wrong! — Case file 009 — Cross Files (Tuesday) -->

In ancient Rome, life expectancy was somewhere between twenty and thirty years. You have heard a number like that before. Maybe in a documentary. Maybe from a friend who was making a point about how lucky we are.

And it conjures a very particular picture. A world of young people. Grandparents were a rarity. You were married at fifteen, a veteran at twenty-five, and an elder statesman at twenty-nine. Then you dropped dead, roughly on schedule, at thirty.

Case file zero zero nine. Subject: you, and your great-great-great-grandparents. Belief: in the ancient world, people were old at thirty, and dead soon after.

I should mention that I am roughly three thousand years old. So I take this one a little personally. In my experience, ancient people were perfectly capable of growing old. Several of them did it just to annoy me. One of them still owes me a library book.

[pause] The problem is not the number. The number is roughly right. The problem is what you think it means.

Exhibit one is the maths. Life expectancy at birth is an average. It takes everyone who was born, including every baby who died in the first week, and calculates how long they lived on average. And averages can be very, very misleading.

Let me show you. Imagine two people. One dies at the age of one. The other lives to seventy-one. Their average lifespan is thirty-six. But nobody in that example died at thirty-six. Not a single person. One died as a baby. The other died old.

It is the same reason the average person has slightly fewer than two legs. Technically true. Completely useless for buying trousers.

Now, scale that up to an entire empire. Historians estimate that in the Roman world, somewhere between a quarter and a third of babies died in their first year. Only around fifty-five to sixty-five per cent survived to the age of five. Perhaps half of everyone born had died by the age of ten. The best evidence we have, census returns from Roman Egypt, points to a life expectancy at birth of just twenty-two to twenty-five years.

That is a staggering amount of loss, and it was real. Behind those numbers are millions of grieving parents. But look at what it does to the average. All those very short lives drag the number right down. A life expectancy at birth of twenty-five does not mean adults died at twenty-five. It means a great many children died before they ever became adults.

So the more useful question is this. If you did survive childhood, how long could you expect to live?

For the Roman Empire, the estimates change dramatically. A Roman child who reached the age of ten could expect, on average, to live several more decades. Into their forties or fifties, on average. And of course, an average includes plenty who went much further.

There is another trap hidden in the evidence. Much of what we know about ancient ages comes from skeletons. And estimating age from bones is hard. Traditional methods have struggled to estimate the age of anyone older than about fifty. A skeleton that belonged to a seventy-year-old can end up in the records simply as older than fifty. Do that across a whole cemetery, and the ancient world starts to look younger than it really was.

Exhibit two. In two thousand seven, two anthropologists, Michael Gurven and Hillard Kaplan, studied hunter-gatherer societies. These are groups living without modern medicine, hospitals, vaccines or antibiotics. Probably the closest thing we have to how humans lived for most of our history. Hard lives, physical work, no pharmacy round the corner.

Life expectancy at birth in these groups ranged from about twenty-one to thirty-seven years. Very low. Exactly the kind of number that gets quoted. Only around fifty-seven per cent of children survived to the age of fifteen.

But then they asked a different question. Among adults, at what age do people most commonly die? The answer was about seventy-two. Across different societies, the range was roughly sixty-eight to seventy-eight.

And someone who reached forty-five could expect, on average, about two more decades of life. The authors concluded that seven decades seems to be the natural lifespan of a human being. Our bodies are built to function well for about seventy years, even without a doctor in sight.

[pause] Exhibit three. The ancients told us themselves.

Long before the Roman Empire, a psalm in the Hebrew Bible put it like this. The days of our years are threescore years and ten. And if by reason of strength they be fourscore years. Threescore and ten is seventy. Fourscore is eighty. That is not a culture that expected to die at thirty. That is a culture that knew exactly what a full life looked like, and wrote it down.

Exhibit four. Look at their politics. In the Roman Republic, after the reforms of the dictator Sulla, you had to be at least thirty just to become a quaestor, the most junior senior office. And to become consul, the highest office in the state, you had to be at least forty-two.

If Romans really died at thirty, then the entire constitution was designed to be run by ghosts. And the word senate itself comes from the Latin senex. Old man. The Romans named their most powerful institution after their elderly.

Roman lawyers even used a table for annuities, regular payments for life, listing how many years of payments to expect at each age. It is attributed to the jurist Ulpian. Historians argue about how it was calculated, and it is not a census. But nobody builds a pension table for a population that is all dead by thirty.

Exhibit five. The individuals. The Greek playwright Sophocles lived to about ninety. Near the end, according to one story, his own sons tried to have him declared incompetent to manage his affairs. He answered the charge in court by reading from the play he was writing at the time, Oedipus at Colonus. The judges, so the story goes, sided with him. The play is still performed. The sons are mostly remembered for losing.

The emperor Augustus, who ruled Rome for over forty years, died at seventy-five. The philosopher Plato reached about eighty. These were not freaks of nature. They were people who were lucky enough, and wealthy enough, to dodge the main killers.

[serious] So what were the main killers? Here, the myth contains a grain of truth, and it is worth being honest about it.

Life was genuinely more dangerous. In the hunter-gatherer data, illness accounted for about seventy per cent of all deaths. Infections that a course of antibiotics would clear today. Violence and accidents made up about twenty per cent. Childbirth was dangerous for mothers. A bad winter, a failed harvest, or a plague could wipe out a town.

So yes, many more people died young, at every age. A thirty-year-old in ancient Rome was in more danger than a thirty-year-old today. The chance of reaching old age was much lower. But the possibility was always there. The human body was not built differently. People did not wear out at thirty. They were killed, mostly by things we can now prevent.

And here is the twist. The biggest change in modern life expectancy did not come from making old people older. It came from keeping babies alive. Clean water, vaccines, better nutrition, antibiotics, and safer childbirth. In about two hundred years, global life expectancy has risen from under thirty to over seventy. A good part of that is simply because far fewer children die. Not all of it, to be fair. Life expectancy has improved at every age. But the children are where the big numbers came from.

We did extend old age a little, too. More people now reach their eighties and nineties than ever before. But the idea that the ancient world had no old people is simply wrong. They had grandparents. They had wise elders. They had grumpy old men complaining about the youth of today. The Bureau has several thousand years of those complaints on file. They are remarkably consistent.

Verdict: wrong.

Ancient people did not die at thirty. Low life expectancy at birth was driven mainly by the deaths of babies and young children. If you survived childhood, you had a real chance of living into your sixties or seventies, and some lived much longer. The natural human lifespan has been about seven decades for a very long time.

The next time someone tells you that people in the past died at thirty, ask them one question. Who, then, was running the Senate?
