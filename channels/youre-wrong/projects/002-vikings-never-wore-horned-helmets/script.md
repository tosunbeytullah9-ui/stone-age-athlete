# Vikings Never Wore Horned Helmets. An Opera Did.

Picture a Viking. Go on. Big beard, big axe, and two big horns sticking out of his helmet. [pause] Those horns were designed in eighteen seventy-five. By a costume designer. For an opera.

Case file zero zero two. Subject: you, and roughly everyone else on Earth. Belief: Vikings went into battle wearing horned helmets.

I have a personal interest in this one. I have met Vikings. I have also met horned helmets. And I can tell you, with some confidence, that the two never met on a battlefield. Mostly, they missed each other by about seventeen centuries.

So let's open the file. And as always, we start with the evidence. Which is where this story gets embarrassing for the helmet.

In March nineteen forty-three, a man in Norway was levelling the ground to build a house, on a farm called Gjermundbu. Instead of foundations, he found a rich warrior's grave from the tenth century. And in it, an iron helmet, broken into pieces. Next to it lay the remains of a mail shirt, the most complete one ever found from the Viking Age. Whoever this was, he was not a farmer who wandered into a fight. He was dressed for war, by the best tailor in the valley.

Archaeologists rebuilt it from nine fragments. A rounded iron cap made of four plates. A fixed guard around the eyes and nose, like a pair of iron spectacles. And on top? Nothing. No horns. Not even a small, apologetic one.

For a long time, this was the only Viking Age helmet that could be put back together. Then, in twenty twenty, an archaeologist from Durham University took a closer look at a rusty helmet that workers had dug up while laying sewage pipes in Yarm, in the north of England, back in the nineteen-fifties. It had been sitting in plain sight for decades. Tenth century. Iron. Round. [pause] Also no horns.

Two helmets. That is the entire collection of nearly complete Viking helmets in the world. Add a handful of fragments, and you reach about five. For a culture that spent centuries raiding and trading across Europe, that is a suspiciously small number of hats.

Historians think the reason is simple. Iron was hard to make, and expensive. A good metal helmet was a luxury, repaired again and again and handed down in families. Many ordinary Vikings probably fought with no metal helmet at all. Leather, wool, and hope.

And while we are correcting things: "Viking" was not even the name of a people. In Old Norse, as the historian Judith Jesch explains, a víkingr was a person on an expedition, usually by sea and in a group. Víking was the activity. You did not grow up a Viking. You went viking, and then, ideally, you came home and fed the cows. Calling everyone in medieval Scandinavia a Viking is a bit like calling everyone in modern Britain a tourist.

And these expedition people travelled far. The Viking Age is usually counted from a raid on the monastery of Lindisfarne, off the coast of England, in seven hundred and ninety-three. Two centuries later, Norse sailors had crossed the whole Atlantic. In twenty twenty-one, scientists used the trace of an ancient solar storm, locked inside tree rings, to date wood cut with metal tools at L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland. The year the trees were cut: one thousand and twenty-one. Exactly one thousand years before the study.

So these were people who could build ships good enough to reach America. People who knew a lot about wind, wood, iron and weight. And somehow, we decided they went into battle with a pair of extra handles bolted to their heads. [pause] I have filed stranger things. Not many.

So if not from the Vikings, where did the horns come from? Let's see who filed the original report.

For most of the nineteenth century, artists already loved Vikings. But they did not give them horns. They gave them wings. Little feathered wings on the sides of the helmet, like a very angry pigeon. In the famous Swedish poem Frithiof's Saga, published in eighteen twenty-five, the hero wears what the poem calls an eagle's helm.

Then, in eighteen seventy-five, a German painter and costume designer named Carl Emil Doepler got a very big job. Richard Wagner was preparing the first complete performance of his opera cycle, The Ring of the Nibelung, in the town of Bayreuth. Doepler had to dress the gods, heroes and villains of Norse myth. And for some of them, he designed helmets with horns.

Not everybody loved it. Wagner's own wife, Cosima, wrote that the costumes made the characters look like Native American chiefs. [pause] She lost that argument. To about a hundred and fifty years of history.

Because the opera premiered in eighteen seventy-six and became a sensation. Illustrators all over Europe copied the look. One popular British adventure novel had shown its Vikings in winged hats through eight illustrated editions. In the ninth, in eighteen ninety, they suddenly grew horns. Nobody explained why. Nobody asked.

The historian Roberta Frank, who traced all of this, found almost no horned Vikings in pictures before the late eighteen-seventies. After that, they were everywhere. On book covers. In cartoons. On sports logos. And eventually, on your head, at a costume party, at two in the morning.

[serious] But here is where the file gets complicated. Because horned helmets are real. Archaeologists have actually dug them up. Just not from Vikings.

In nineteen forty-two, a peat worker cutting turf in a bog near Veksø, in Denmark, found two bronze helmets. Each had two long, curving horns, a curled beak, and a pair of staring eyes. Beautiful. Completely impractical for a fight.

In twenty twenty-two, Danish researchers radiocarbon-dated material from the helmets. They had been placed in that bog around nine hundred BC. Almost three thousand years ago. Nearly seventeen hundred years before the Viking Age began. One of the archaeologists described the Viking connection, and I quote, as "nonsense."

I was there, by the way. Not in the bog. At the ceremonies. Those helmets were for rituals and processions, not for battle. Nobody sensible goes into a fight wearing a helmet that offers the enemy two convenient handles.

And to be fair to the costume designer, he did not invent horns out of thin air. Even the Vikings' own art has a few horned figures. A ninth-century tapestry from the famous Oseberg ship burial in Norway shows people in horned headgear, probably in a ritual procession. But no helmet like that has ever been found, and as the National Museum of Denmark points out, no written source from the time describes Vikings wearing horns into battle. Nineteenth-century collectors kept finding ancient horned figures. A golden horn from Denmark. Little bronze statues. A metal plate from Sweden showing a dancer in a horned headdress. Those finds were real. The mistake was taking a thousand years of very different people, and gluing all of their headgear onto one Viking.

While the file is open, one more correction, free of charge. The other half of the costume is the dirt. Vikings are usually pictured as filthy. Archaeologists keep finding the opposite. Combs, in the graves of both men and women. Tweezers. Ear cleaners. Toothpicks. In Old Norse, the word for Saturday literally means bath day.

One English chronicle, written long after the Viking raids, even complained that Danish men combed their hair every day, bathed every Saturday and changed their clothes regularly. It was written as a complaint. In our archive, it is filed under jealousy.

So, back to the file. Vikings in horned helmets. The real Viking helmets we have are round, iron and hornless. The real horned helmets we have are bronze, ceremonial, and about seventeen centuries too early. And the picture in your head was designed in eighteen seventy-five, for an opera.

Verdict: wrong. [pause] Wrong helmet. Wrong century. Wrong millennium. Right opera.

Now, a filing note you can actually use. How to catch a costume pretending to be history.

One. Ask for the oldest source. Not the most famous picture. The oldest one. If the horns first appear in the eighteen-seventies, they are an eighteen-seventies idea.

Two. Look at what was dug up, not what was drawn. Paintings show what artists believed. Graves show what people owned.

Three. When something looks a little too perfect for the story, ask who needed the story. Here, Roberta Frank points at a stage production and the national pride of the late nineteenth century. History rarely gets this dramatic on its own. It needs a costume department.
