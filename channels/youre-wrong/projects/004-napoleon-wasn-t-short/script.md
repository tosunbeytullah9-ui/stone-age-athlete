# Napoleon Wasn't Short
<!-- You're Wrong! — Case file 004 — History Files (Friday) -->

Napoleon Bonaparte. Emperor of the French. Conqueror of half of Europe. And, as everyone knows, a tiny, furious little man in a very large hat.

He even has a psychological condition named after him. Short man, big temper, desperate to prove something. You have probably used it yourself, about a colleague. Quietly. Out of earshot.

Case file zero zero four. Subject: an emperor, and everyone who ever laughed at his height. Belief: Napoleon was a tiny man with a temper to match.

I should declare an interest. I was on Saint Helena in eighteen twenty-one. Not socially. I was there for the paperwork. And the paperwork, as usual, tells a rather different story from the one you were taught.

Let us begin with the body. Napoleon died on the fifth of May, eighteen twenty-one, in exile, on a small rock in the South Atlantic. His physician, Francesco Antommarchi, measured him. His valet, Louis Marchand, helped. And the number they wrote down was five feet, two inches.

There it is. Five foot two. Case closed, you might think. A small man. The cartoonists were right. Off you go.

[pause] Except for one tiny detail. Those were not your feet, and they were not your inches.

The measurement was taken in old French units. Pieds and pouces. And a French inch, a pouce, was about two point seven centimetres. An English inch is two point five four. The French foot was longer too. Same words, different ruler.

So do the arithmetic, which nobody in London bothered to do. Five pieds, two pouces and four lignes, the last little bit, comes to about one hundred and sixty-eight centimetres. One metre sixty-eight, or roughly five foot six in English measure.

And there is a second opinion. An English upholsterer named Andrew Darling also measured the body, with an English tape. He wrote down five feet seven inches. About one metre seventy. A British witness, with a British ruler. Not exactly a member of the Napoleon fan club.

Back in eighteen oh two, a visitor named John Leslie Foster saw the First Consul and described him as about five foot seven. That was before anyone needed him to be small.

The confusion never quite went away. In nineteen sixty-three, a scholarly journal in France printed his height as one metre sixty-four. A typo. The next issue had to correct it to one metre sixty-eight. Even the correction of the myth needed a correction. I filed both.

Now, five foot six or seven is not tall today. But Napoleon was not born today. He was born in seventeen sixty-nine, and in his day he was roughly average. Some historians put him slightly above the average Frenchman of his time. Not a giant. Not a dwarf. A perfectly ordinary-sized man with a perfectly extraordinary ego.

[pause] So where did the tiny emperor come from? Three suspects. A ruler, a bodyguard, and a man with a pen.

Suspect one, the ruler. We have covered it. French inches were read as English inches, and the man lost about ten centimetres overnight. Without even dieting.

Suspect two, the company he kept. Napoleon's Imperial Guard was the elite of his army, and it was picked partly for size. A grenadier of the Old Guard had to stand at least five foot ten. Roughly one metre seventy-eight. Then they put tall bearskin hats on top.

Now stand a man of one metre sixty-eight in the middle of that. In a plain green colonel's coat, while his marshals wear feathers and gold braid. Of course he looks small. Put anyone next to a basketball team and they look like a garden gnome.

And then there is the nickname. Le petit caporal. The little corporal. Surely that settles it?

It does not. His soldiers used it, and in French, petit is often affection, not measurement. It is what you call someone you are fond of. The legend says the name was born at the Battle of Lodi, in seventeen ninety-six, when the young general aimed the cannons himself, like a common corporal. Lovely story. Historians point out there is no contemporary evidence for it. Either way, it was a pet name, not a tape measure.

Which brings us to suspect three. The real culprit. A Londoner named James Gillray.

Gillray was the most famous caricaturist in Britain. His prints hung in the window of a shop on Saint James's Street, and crowds pressed against the glass to see the latest one. From seventeen ninety-seven, he was reportedly receiving money from the government of William Pitt. And he never once saw Napoleon in person.

When the Peace of Amiens collapsed in eighteen oh three and war returned, Gillray went to work. He drew Napoleon as a spoiled, screaming child, with a giant hat, enormous boots and a sword longer than he was. And he gave him a name. Little Boney.

The temper came from real life, slightly enlarged. In March eighteen oh three, Napoleon lost his patience with the British ambassador, Lord Whitworth, in front of a room full of diplomats. The London papers reported that he had threatened to invade with four or five hundred thousand men. Gillray drew him mid-tantrum, the table kicked over, the globe on the floor. Small, loud, and dangerous. A brand was born.

In June eighteen oh three, he published The King of Brobdingnag and Gulliver. King George the Third, enormous, peers through a spyglass at a tiny Napoleon standing in the palm of his hand.

In eighteen oh five came his masterpiece, The Plumb-pudding in Danger. Napoleon and Pitt sit at a table, carving up the globe like a Christmas pudding. Pitt is tall and lanky. Napoleon is about half his size. It is often called the most famous political cartoon of all time.

Other artists joined in. In eighteen fourteen, George Cruikshank drew Snuffing Out Boney. A miniature Napoleon rises out of a candlestick, and a Cossack puts him out like a candle flame.

Napoleon noticed. He reportedly complained that Gillray did more to bring him down than all the armies of Europe. In eighteen oh six, he even demanded that the British government censor its cartoonists. The British government, with great politeness, ignored him.

To be fair to the British, Napoleon was running his own image factory. He asked the painter Jacques-Louis David to show him calm, on a fiery steed, crossing the Alps. David delivered a rearing stallion, a billowing cloak and a heroic finger pointing at destiny. Napoleon refused to sit for it. David used a bust for the face and his own son for the pose.

Fifty years later, the painter Paul Delaroche painted the same crossing more honestly. Napoleon, wrapped against the cold, on a mule. Two sides, two sets of pictures, two lies. One made him a giant. The other made him a toy. Guess which one stuck.

[serious] Which leaves the last piece of evidence. The thing named after him. The Napoleon complex.

The idea is simple. Short men are more aggressive, because they are compensating. It sounds plausible. And it is exactly the kind of thing you can believe for a whole lifetime without ever testing it.

In two thousand seven, researchers at the University of Central Lancashire tested it. They paired men in a stick-fencing exercise, and one of them deliberately rapped his partner on the knuckles. Then they watched who lost his temper. The short men mostly stayed calm. It was the taller men who were more likely to hit back.

In two thousand eighteen, a team at the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam looked again. In a money-sharing game, shorter men did keep a little more for themselves when playing against a taller man. But they were not more likely to become physically aggressive. Slightly more strategic, perhaps. Angrier, no.

One of the earlier researchers, Mike Eslea, had a theory. When a short man gets angry, people blame his height, because his height is the first thing they notice. When a tall man gets angry, he is just a man having a bad day.

[pause] Verdict: wrong.

Napoleon Bonaparte was about one metre sixty-eight to one metre seventy. Roughly average for his time. He was shrunk by a unit conversion, surrounded by giants in bearskin hats, and drawn as a toddler by a man who never met him.

He conquered most of Europe. He rewrote French law. He crowned himself emperor. And two hundred years later, the one thing everyone remembers about him is a height he never had.

There is a lesson in that, and it is not about height. If you want to shrink someone, you do not need to beat their armies. You just need a good cartoonist, and enough time.

I am filing this one under propaganda, cross-referenced with measurement errors. It is a very thick folder.
