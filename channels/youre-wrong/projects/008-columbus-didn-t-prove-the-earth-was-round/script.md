# Columbus Didn't Prove the Earth Was Round
<!-- You're Wrong! — Case file 008 — History Files (Friday) -->

Fourteen ninety-two. A brave Italian sailor stands before a council of frowning, bearded scholars. He insists the Earth is round. They insist it is flat, and that his ships will sail off the edge of the world. He sails anyway. And he proves them all wrong.

It is one of the great stories of the triumph of reason over ignorance. Children have learned it for nearly two hundred years. There is only one problem. Almost none of it happened.

Case file zero zero eight. Subject: you, and most school textbooks. Belief: people in fourteen ninety-two thought the Earth was flat, until Columbus proved them wrong.

I was in Europe in the fourteen nineties. I attended several very long meetings about Columbus. Nobody, at any of them, mentioned an edge.

Let us begin long before Columbus. Around the fifth century before Christ, Greek thinkers were already describing a spherical Earth. Aristotle pointed out that during a lunar eclipse, the shadow the Earth casts on the Moon is always curved. Only a ball casts a round shadow from every angle.

And then, in the third century before Christ, a scholar named Eratosthenes did something astonishing. He was the chief librarian of the great Library of Alexandria, which is a very good job for a man with questions. He measured it.

He knew that at noon on the summer solstice, in the city of Syene, in southern Egypt, the Sun stood directly overhead. Upright sticks cast no shadow. Sunlight reached the bottom of a well. But at the same moment in Alexandria, further north, the Sun was slightly off to one side. Shadows appeared.

He measured the angle of the shadow in Alexandria. It was about seven degrees. One fiftieth of a full circle. And the distance between the two cities was about five thousand stadia. So the whole Earth, he reasoned, must be fifty times that. Around two hundred and fifty thousand stadia.

Depending on exactly how long his stadion was, that is remarkably close to the real figure of about forty thousand kilometres. With a stick, a shadow, and some honest arithmetic. Seventeen centuries before Columbus.

[pause] So the Greeks knew. Surely the Middle Ages forgot? This is the second half of the myth. The Dark Ages, when the Church supposedly made everyone believe the world was a pancake.

They did not. Around the year twelve thirty, a scholar named John of Holywood, known as Sacrobosco, wrote a textbook called On the Sphere. It clearly describes the Earth as a sphere. It became the standard astronomy textbook in European universities for centuries. When printing arrived, it was printed again and again, in more than eighty editions.

Every educated person who went to university in the late Middle Ages learned from a book whose title was, quite literally, the sphere.

Look at medieval kings. In their portraits, they often hold a golden ball topped with a cross. It is called an orb, and it represents the world. They were not holding a golden plate.

Historians have looked very hard for medieval flat-Earthers. They found a couple. An early Christian writer named Lactantius, around the year three hundred and ten. And a sixth-century merchant and monk called Cosmas, who wrote a strange book describing the world as a sort of box. Both were outliers. Even at the time, hardly anyone took them seriously. In fifteen forty-three, Copernicus himself mentioned Lactantius in his great book, mainly to laugh at him. The historian Stephen Jay Gould put it bluntly. There never was a period of flat Earth darkness among scholars.

[pause] So what was the argument about in fourteen ninety-two? It was real. It was heated. And Columbus lost it. Because he was wrong.

Nobody disputed the shape. They disputed the size.

Columbus wanted to reach Asia by sailing west. For that plan to work, the Earth had to be small, and Asia had to be close. So he chose his numbers accordingly.

He had some encouragement. In fourteen seventy-four, a Florentine astronomer named Paolo Toscanelli had suggested to the King of Portugal that sailing west would be a shortcut to Asia. The king was not interested. Columbus reportedly wrote to Toscanelli, and was encouraged. A man who wants to hear yes will usually find someone to say it.

He took the medieval Arab astronomer Alfraganus, who had measured the length of a degree in Arabic miles. Columbus seems to have assumed these were the shorter Roman miles used in Italy. A different mile, a smaller planet. He also preferred the more modest estimates of the ancient geographer Ptolemy to the larger, more accurate figure of Eratosthenes. Every choice shrank the world.

His conclusion? From the Canary Islands to Japan was about two thousand four hundred nautical miles. Manageable, he thought.

The real distance is about ten thousand six hundred nautical miles. More than four times further. No ship of his day could have carried enough food and water to cross that much open ocean.

And the experts knew it. In Portugal, the advisers of King John the Second looked at his plan and turned it down. In Spain, a commission led by the queen's confessor, Hernando de Talavera, also found it implausible. Their objection was not that he would fall off. It was that he would starve. They thought Asia was much further away than he claimed.

They were right. He was wrong. If the Americas had not been sitting in the way, an entirely unexpected continent, roughly where he thought Japan would be, Columbus and his crews would have died somewhere in the middle of the ocean. He was not saved by being right. He was saved by being lucky.

And Columbus was not exactly a reliable authority on the shape of the Earth anyway. On his third voyage, in fourteen ninety-eight, he wrote that the world was not perfectly round at all. It was shaped like a pear, he said, very round except where the stalk grows. He believed he had found the stalk. The Bureau has that letter. It is in the drawer marked confident.

And here is a lovely detail. In fourteen ninety-two, the very year Columbus sailed, a merchant named Martin Behaim finished a globe in Nuremberg. A round model of the Earth. It is the oldest surviving globe in the world. It has no Americas on it, of course. Nobody in Europe knew they were there yet. But it is very definitely round.

The roundness would be demonstrated in the most practical way possible thirty years later. In fifteen nineteen, five ships and about two hundred and seventy men set out under Ferdinand Magellan. Three years later, one ship came home, having sailed all the way around. Eighteen men were on board. Proving the obvious has never been cheap.

[serious] So where did the flat Earth story come from? Not from the Middle Ages. From the nineteenth century.

In eighteen twenty-eight, the American writer Washington Irving, the man who gave us The Legend of Sleepy Hollow, published a biography of Columbus. It was beautifully written, enormously popular, and widely treated as scholarship. It was also partly invented.

Irving described a dramatic council at Salamanca, where narrow-minded churchmen quoted scripture to argue against a round Earth. That scene is largely fiction. But it was a very good scene. Painters painted it. Schoolbooks repeated it.

Then the story found a job. Later in the century, two influential books, by John William Draper in eighteen seventy-four, and Andrew Dickson White in eighteen ninety-six, presented history as a long war between science and religion. The flat Earth was perfect ammunition. The medieval Church, they implied, had kept people believing the world was flat, until brave thinkers like Columbus set them free.

It was a satisfying story. It made the past look foolish, and the present look clever. Which, in my experience, is exactly the kind of story that people never bother to check.

In nineteen ninety-one, the historian Jeffrey Burton Russell traced the whole thing in a book called Inventing the Flat Earth. The myth, he showed, was not a medieval belief at all. It was a modern invention about the medieval world.

Verdict: wrong.

Columbus did not prove the Earth was round. Educated Europeans had known it was round for well over a thousand years. The real argument in fourteen ninety-two was about size, and Columbus was on the wrong side of it. His critics were the ones doing the better maths.

The irony is rather delicious. The man celebrated for defeating ignorance was the one who got the numbers wrong. And the scholars mocked for centuries as flat-Earth fools were, in fact, correct.
