# Gladiators Rarely Fought to the Death
<!-- You're Wrong! — Case file 006 — History Files (Friday) -->

The Colosseum. Fifty thousand screaming Romans. Two gladiators circle each other in the sand. One falls. The winner looks up at the emperor. The emperor pauses, and turns his thumb down. Death.

You have seen this scene a hundred times. In paintings, in films, in at least one very loud video game. Gladiators fought to the death. That was the whole point.

Case file zero zero six. Subject: you, and every film you have ever seen about Rome. Belief: gladiators fought to the death.

I attended a few games in my time. Professionally, of course. Someone had to count. And I can tell you that the arena was brutal, it was bloody, and it was not, most of the time, a fight to the death.

First, who were they? Many were slaves, or prisoners of war. But by the first century, a surprising number were free men who signed contracts with gladiator schools of their own accord. Desperate men, former soldiers, and, to the horror of polite society, the occasional nobleman. They came for money, and for fame. On a wall in Pompeii, someone scratched that one fighter was the delight of all the girls. Another, apparently, caught the girls at night in his net.

Exhibit one. The money. With the Romans, there was always money. A gladiator was not a random man thrown onto the sand. He was a trained professional. He was bought, housed, fed, trained and given medical care by a manager called a lanista. That cost a fortune. And a dead gladiator was a fortune lost.

We even have the legal paperwork. A Roman jurist named Gaius, writing in the second century, discussed a contract for hiring out gladiators. The fee was twenty for each man who came back unharmed. And one thousand for each man who was killed or maimed.

Twenty, against one thousand. Fifty times more. If you were the man paying for the show, every death was an extremely expensive decision. And if you were the lanista, you wanted your property to walk out of that arena on its own legs.

Fights were not a free-for-all, either. Every bout had a referee, sometimes two. A gladiator who was beaten, or badly wounded, could raise a finger and appeal for mercy. Then the fight stopped, and the decision passed to the man who was paying for the games. Often, that was the emperor.

And very often the answer was mercy. Historians estimate that somewhere between one in five and one in ten bouts ended with a death. Put the other way around, most fights ended with both men still alive. Some even ended in a draw, with both fighters leaving the arena standing, to applause.

Let me show you a career. In Sicily, there is a tombstone for a gladiator named Flamma. He died at thirty. It records his fights. Thirty-four bouts. Twenty-one wins. Nine draws. And four defeats.

Four defeats. And he survived every one of them. He lost four times in front of a crowd, and he was spared four times. That is not what a fight to the death looks like. That looks like a career.

You may have read online that Flamma was offered his freedom four times, and refused it every time. A lovely story. The tombstone says nothing of the kind. That number four is not four offers of freedom. It is four times he was beaten, and spared. It was put up by a comrade-in-arms named Delicatus, for a man described as Syrian by birth. Even the myth-busting has myths. I have a separate drawer for those.

[pause] Exhibit two. The bones.

In nineteen ninety-three, archaeologists in Ephesus, in what is now Turkey, found a cemetery. It belonged to gladiators, from around the second and third centuries. Researchers from Vienna and Bern studied the remains of at least sixty-eight people.

Eleven of them had old head wounds that had healed well. These men had been hurt, treated, and had gone back to work. Someone had stitched them up. Someone wanted them back in the arena.

The skeletons also showed something else. Not one of them had a pile of fresh wounds. Each fatal injury was a single blow, from a single weapon. No frenzied hacking. The researchers concluded that the rules of combat were strictly enforced. This was a regulated sport, not a massacre.

To be fair, the bones also show the dark side. Ten of the men had died from a single fresh blow to the head. Some researchers think a few of these were finishing strikes, delivered to men the crowd had already condemned. Rules, yes. Gentle, no.

And the medical care was serious. In the year one hundred and fifty-seven, a young doctor named Galen took a job looking after the gladiators at Pergamon. Galen would later become the most famous physician of the ancient world, and doctor to the emperor Marcus Aurelius. He called their wounds windows into the body.

According to Galen himself, only five gladiators died during his time in the post. Under his predecessor, the figure was sixty. Five, against sixty. Gladiators had their own trauma surgeon. And a good one.

Now. Let us deal with the thumbs.

That famous image, the crowd with their thumbs turned down, comes from a painting. It is called Pollice Verso, which means with a turned thumb. It was painted by Jean-Léon Gérôme, in eighteen seventy-two. It shows a defeated gladiator lying in the sand, while the spectators, including the Vestal Virgins, point their thumbs at the ground.

It was a magnificent painting. It was so magnificent that more than a century later, it helped persuade a film director to make a film called Gladiator. And so the thumbs went around the world, again.

Here is the problem. The Romans did use a thumb gesture. The poet Juvenal mentions a crowd condemning a man with a turned thumb. But nobody in antiquity tells us which way the thumb actually turned.

Historians are still arguing. One suggestion is that a thumb pointed up meant death. And that a thumb pressed down against a closed fist meant mercy. Let him live. If that is right, the thumbs-up you use to approve a holiday photo might once have meant kill him.

The crowd also had a simpler method. Shouting. Romans yelled let him go, or, less charmingly, slay him. Waving cloths may have helped too. Subtle people, the Romans.

[pause] And then, the salute. Ave Caesar, morituri te salutant. Hail Caesar, those who are about to die salute you. Every gladiator film has someone bellow it.

In all of Roman literature, it appears in connection with a single event. In the year fifty-two, the emperor Claudius staged a mock naval battle on a lake. The fighters were not gladiators. They were condemned criminals. And they called out the line, apparently hoping to be spared.

Claudius replied, or not. The men decided he had pardoned them, and refused to fight. It took some persuading to get them into the boats. It was a one-time plea from desperate prisoners. Historians have found no evidence that it was a regular gladiator salute.

[serious] Now, in fairness to the Romans' reputation, the arena was a terrible place. People did die there, and some of them had no chance at all. Condemned criminals were executed in the arena, sometimes by animals. Prisoners of war were sent in to die. In the earlier days of the Republic, some fights were openly held without mercy, until Augustus banned them.

And let us not get sentimental. Most gladiators still died young, many by their mid-twenties. A one-in-ten chance of dying every time you go to work adds up quickly. You would not take that job. Plenty of Romans did.

So death was there. It was just not the job description. For a trained gladiator, the arena was a dangerous profession. Closer to a modern combat sport than to an execution.

One last detail, for the fitness fans. The gladiators of Ephesus ate mostly grain and beans. Barley, especially. Pliny the Elder says people called them barley men. And their bones contained unusually high levels of strontium, which suggests they drank a tonic made from plant ashes, to help them recover. Even the diet was managed. You do not do that for a man you expect to lose by Thursday.

Verdict: wrong.

Gladiators did not usually fight to the death. Most bouts ended with both men alive. They were expensive professionals, with referees, rules, surgeons and a managed diet. Defeat usually meant humiliation, not execution. And the thumbs-down came from a painter, some eighteen centuries later.

The truth is less cinematic. But it is somehow more human. A man named Flamma lost four times, walked out four times, and fought again.
